About Owls

Owls are birds that are most active at night and hunt mice, insects, and other small animals. ￼ They have big forward-facing eyes and amazing hearing; the ring of feathers on their face works like a sound catcher, and their soft-edged wings let them fly very quietly. They can twist their heads almost all the way around to look without moving their bodies. Owls live on every continent except Antarctica and come in many sizes, from tiny elf owls to very large eagle-owls. Their sharp beaks and claws help them grab food, and their patterned feathers help them hide among trees or snow. Fun fact: a group of owls is called a “parliament”.

Arctic Bird Spotlight

Snowy Owl (Bubo scandiacus)

A powerful white owl from the Arctic tundra. In some winters, it flies south and can be seen in open places like fields, beaches, and even big airports.

At a glance

Length: ~20–28 in
Wingspan: ~4.2–4.8 ft
Weight: ~3.5–6.5 lb
Color: mostly white
Active by day & night
Favorite prey: lemmings

Quick Facts

Where it lives: Summers on the Arctic tundra; in winter many move south to open fields, beaches, and coastal marshes.

How it hunts: Watches from a high spot or hovers, then flies fast to seize prey with strong talons.

What it eats: Mostly lemmings; also rabbits, voles, ducks, geese, and sometimes fish or carrion.

Why it’s special

  • Often active in daylight; males get whiter with age while females keep more dark spots.
  • Some winters bring wide-ranging irruptions far south.

Look Closely: Identification

  • Face & eyes: round head; bright yellow eyes.
  • Plumage: mostly white; females retain more brown/gray barring.
  • Size: large owl (~2 ft long; wingspan close to 5 ft).
  • Feathering: thick feathers on legs and toes for cold.

Field Tip

From far away, a sitting Snowy Owl can resemble a small snow pile. Use binoculars and keep your distance.

Home & Range

Nests on raised spots across the Arctic tundra. In winter many move to open land farther south (prairies, farm fields, beaches, even airports). Large southward movements (“irruptions”) are linked to prey cycles.

Nesting

  • Shallow scrape on the ground with wide visibility.
  • Open landscapes preferred for spotting danger and prey.

Food & Hunting

Favorite food: lemmings; also rabbits, voles, ducks and geese when available.

Hunting style: watchful perch-and-dash or brief hovering; keen vision and hearing—can take prey through snow cover.

Behavior

  • Active day or night; often dawn/dusk peaks.
  • Perches on dunes, mounds, fence posts, or ground.
  • Strong wingbeats built for long flights over open land and sea ice.

Family Life

  • Eggs: usually 3–11 (more in food-rich years).
  • Incubation: ~1 month by the female; male delivers prey.
  • Development: chicks depart the nest at 2–3 weeks, fly well at ~7 weeks, and remain dependent for weeks.

Voices

Generally quiet in winter; on breeding grounds gives deep hoots, croaks, whistles; chicks squeak then call loudly as they grow.

Conservation

Facing change in Arctic habitats and fluctuating prey cycles. Protect wide, open habitats; reduce disturbance; observe from a distance.

American Barn Owl (Tyto furcata)

A pale, heart-faced night hunter that helps farmers by eating mice and voles. What it looks like, where it lives, how it hunts, and how you can help.

At a glance

Length: ~13–16 in
Wingspan: ~3.3–4.1 ft
Weight: ~1.0–1.6 lb
Color: pale buff & gray
Active mostly at night
Favorite prey: voles & mice

Quick Facts

Where it lives: Open areas—farms, fields, marsh edges; roosts and nests in quiet buildings, tree cavities, cliffs, and nest boxes.

How it hunts: Flies low over fields, listening; drops quickly on the faintest rustle.

What it eats: Mostly small mammals (voles, mice); occasionally shrews and small rats.

Why it’s special

  • Legendary silent flight from fringed feathers.
  • Extreme hearing from slightly offset ears and facial disc.
  • Valuable natural pest control for farms and orchards.

Look Closely: Identification

  • Face & eyes: white, heart-shaped face; dark eyes; long legs.
  • Plumage: back pale brown to gray; underparts white to warm buff with fine spots.
  • Size: medium owl; short, squarish tail; long broad wings.

Field Tip

At night a barn owl looks ghost-pale and doesn’t hoot—it gives a long, harsh scream. Watch for a buoyant, moth-like flight.

Home & Range

Found across most of the Americas (including Hawai‘i). Prefers open country with grassy edges; uses barns, silos, bridges, or nest boxes for shelter.

Nesting

  • Clutch usually 3–8, laid every 2–3 days; incubation ~30 days.
  • Young fledge ~55–65 days; parents both feed the brood.
  • Nests are simple—often pellets and a shallow depression.

Food & Hunting

Diet: chiefly voles and mice; prey swallowed whole and later egested as dry pellets.

Method: quartering flights a few meters above ground; can take prey by sound alone.

Behavior

  • Strongly nocturnal; peak activity around dusk and dawn.
  • Often wanders widely when young before settling.
  • Roosts in cavities and quiet structures by day.

Family Life

  • Monogamous pairs; male delivers most prey during incubation.
  • Asynchronous hatching can produce different-sized chicks in one brood.

Voices

  • Long, loud scream.
  • Hisses, grunts, and beak clicks when alarmed.

Conservation

  • Avoid second-generation rodenticides (secondary poisoning risk).
  • Provide safe nest boxes away from busy roads.
  • Maintain grassy margins and field edges for hunting.
Widespread Raptor Spotlight

Great Horned Owl (Bubo virginianus)

A powerful, adaptable predator native to the Americas. Recognizable by bold ear tufts, yellow eyes, deep hoots, and formidable talons.

At a glance

Length: ~17–25 in
Wingspan: ~3–5 ft
Weight: ~2.7–3.5 lb (♀ larger)
Color: mottled brown/gray; white throat
Active mostly at night
Favorite prey: rabbits, mice & voles

Quick Facts

Where it lives: Throughout most of North and South America, from subarctic forests to deserts, grasslands, wetlands, mountains, and some parks in towns.

How it hunts: Perch-and-pounce; short, silent flights; can locate prey by sound alone.

What it eats: Mainly mammals (rabbits/hares, rats, mice, voles), also birds, reptiles, amphibians, and invertebrates; among the few regular skunk predators.

Why it’s special

  • Earliest nesting of any large North American raptor—often eggs in winter.
  • Remarkable grip strength (≈300 psi) and near-silent flight surfaces.
  • Extremely adaptable; one of the most widely distributed true owls in the Americas.

Look Closely: Identification

  • Face & eyes: large yellow eyes; bold facial disc with dark brackets; prominent feather “horns” (plumicorns).
  • Plumage: cryptic mottled brown/gray; white throat patch that may flare during calling.
  • Build: heavy, barrel-chested body; broad wings; feathered legs and feet.

Field Tip

Listen for a deep hoo-hoo-hoo hoo hoo (female slightly higher, rising at the end). Daytime roosts may be revealed by mobs of crows.

Home & Range

Breeds from northern Canada and Alaska through the U.S., Central America, and much of South America, using edges where woods meet open hunting grounds; occurs from sea level to >10,000 ft in the Andes.

Nesting

  • Uses abandoned stick nests, cavities, cliffs, or structures rather than building new nests.
  • Clutch 1–5 eggs (typically 2); incubation about 30–37 days.
  • Young leave the nest at 6–9 weeks, fly at ~9–10 weeks; often remain with parents until autumn.

Food & Hunting

Diet breadth: >500 prey species recorded across the Americas; mammals dominate. Pellets large (≈3–4 in long).

Method: watch from perch; swift pounce; can carry or cache large prey; often dismembers large items.

Behavior

  • Mostly nocturnal; roosts in dense trees, hollows, or rocky niches.
  • Pairs defend territories year-round; unmated “floaters” linger along borders.
  • Crows frequently mob roosting birds during the day.

Family Life

  • Female larger and does most incubation; male supplies prey.
  • Young vocalize with hisses/screeches; remain dependent for weeks after first flights.

Voices

Deep, resonant hoots in 4–5 syllables; also growls, screeches, and beak-clicks—especially at the nest or during disputes.

Conservation

  • Status: Least Concern globally due to broad range and adaptability.
  • Local risks: habitat loss/fragmentation, vehicle collisions, pesticides; disease (e.g., West Nile virus) reported in some areas.
